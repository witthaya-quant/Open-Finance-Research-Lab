# OFRL Foundation, Vision, Mission & Principles

This document sets out why Open Finance Research Lab (OFRL) exists, what it is working towards, and how people with different backgrounds and methods can develop useful work together.

It is the guiding philosophy for the lab. The Research Charter, Research Standards, and project-specific specifications turn this philosophy into working requirements. [GOVERNANCE.md](GOVERNANCE.md) defines how authority, accountability, decisions, and contributor responsibilities operate while OFRL remains under the Founder's personal GitHub account. A GitHub Organization is optional and will be considered only if a concrete operational need arises.

## 1. Why OFRL exists

I started OFRL because I believe finance and business move forward when people are willing to share what they know, examine how things work, and improve what can be improved.

People who study finance, economics, mathematics, statistics, accounting, or business often begin with many of the same foundations. We learn established concepts, equations, and frameworks. Black–Scholes, DCF, portfolio theory, accounting principles, and statistical methods are examples of knowledge that many of us can study and understand.

But shared foundations do not mean that everyone will apply them in the same way. In real work, people develop their own methods, assumptions, techniques, workflows, and practical judgement. Their choices are shaped by the problem they are solving, the market or business they work in, the information available, their constraints, and what they are trying to achieve. Over time, each person develops a way of doing things that works for their own context.

I do not see those differences as a reason to decide who is better, whose method is automatically superior, or who has the final answer. I see them as an opportunity to learn from one another.

I want OFRL to make it possible for people to bring forward methods and ideas they are able and entitled to share, explain how they work, test them, compare them with alternatives, and help develop them further. The point is not to prove that one person is smarter than another. The point is to understand what a method can do, where it is useful, where it falls short, and what we can build or improve from it.

## 2. Vision

**To build an open, evolving body of financial knowledge, research methods, and practical tools that people can inspect, test, use, adapt, and improve together.**

OFRL aims to be a place where different perspectives can contribute to better work. Knowledge should not stop at discussion or remain useful only to the person who first developed it. Where rights and practical constraints allow, research should be documented and developed so that others can learn from it and build on it.

## 3. Mission

**To bring together people with different experiences and areas of expertise to investigate financial and business problems, develop and examine alternative methods, and turn useful research into reproducible models, software, and practical resources.**

The work includes:

- Making financial, mathematical, statistical, and business concepts understandable and traceable.
- Developing alternative approaches rather than assuming that an established method is the only possible approach.
- Testing assumptions, calculations, implementations, and results.
- Comparing methods under clearly stated objectives, conditions, and constraints.
- Identifying what works, what does not, what remains uncertain, and what should be improved.
- Publishing reusable research, code, models, documentation, and lawful data resources where appropriate.
- Giving other people a sound starting point from which to learn, adapt, challenge, and develop further work.

OFRL is not trying to settle every debate or force everyone into one methodology. It is trying to make the work better and more useful.

## 4. Foundational beliefs

### 4.1 Common foundations, different applications

Many people in finance and business learn the same foundations through degrees, professional qualifications, certificates, or independent study. We become familiar with common concepts, frameworks, and equations. Black–Scholes is one example: people may learn the same model and then develop different ways to apply, extend, parameterise, test, or combine it.

In practice, every person or team may develop its own “secret sauce”—the assumptions, techniques, information, processes, and judgement refined through experience for a particular objective. Those differences are worth understanding. They are not, by themselves, a reason to rank the people behind them or assume that one approach must be universally superior.

A familiar formula is a starting point, not a complete answer to every real-world problem. I want us to understand the shared foundation, examine how each method applies it, and learn what can be used, adapted, improved, or developed further.

### 4.2 Different methods deserve examination, not automatic ranking

A method should not be dismissed simply because it is unfamiliar, comes from a less recognised person, or differs from the current approach. It should be understood and examined on its merits.

Equally, a method should not be accepted merely because it is novel, popular, confidently presented, or developed by someone with an impressive title. Respect for different perspectives does not remove the need for scrutiny.

### 4.3 Open-minded, but evidence-driven

OFRL welcomes disagreement and competing explanations. It does not treat every claim as equally supported or every implementation as equally sound.

We examine methods against the problem they are intended to address. Relevant considerations may include mathematical and accounting correctness, assumptions, data quality, implementation integrity, statistical evidence, robustness, economic or operational constraints, reproducibility, and practical usefulness.

A method may be suitable for one context and unsuitable for another. Where evidence is insufficient, the correct conclusion is that more work is needed—not that the claim has been proven.

### 4.4 Evaluate the work, not the status of the person

Titles, qualifications, seniority, reputation, and years of experience can provide context, but they do not replace an explanation or evidence.

The same standards of reasoning and review should apply to founders, experienced practitioners, researchers, students, and first-time contributors. Criticism should address the method, assumptions, implementation, or claim—not a person's identity or status.

### 4.5 Practical value matters

Research should help us understand something, solve a problem, make a method more reliable, or create something that can be used and developed further.

Not every project needs to become a commercial product. However, we should be able to explain what the work contributes, what it is suitable for, and how another person could use or extend it.

### 4.6 Transparency makes collaboration possible

People cannot reliably examine or improve work when important assumptions, definitions, data sources, or implementation details are hidden.

We make material decisions visible, document how results were produced, and distinguish observed facts, assumptions, estimates, and conclusions.

### 4.7 Limitations and failure are part of progress

A method that fails under a well-designed test can teach us something important. Negative, inconclusive, and conflicting results should not be hidden to protect an idea or the reputation of its author.

We would rather understand a limitation than make a claim stronger than the evidence allows.

### 4.8 Build on work with respect and attribution

Research advances when people can learn from existing work without erasing the people who contributed to it. We acknowledge sources, preserve attribution, distinguish original contributions from adaptations, and respect the rights attached to software, data, and research materials.

Open collaboration does not require anyone to disclose confidential information, employer-owned work, client material, or private methods they do not have the right to share.

## 5. Principles in practice

The following principles guide research, implementation, discussion, and review at OFRL.

1. **Understand before judging.** Explain the problem, objective, assumptions, and context before comparing approaches.
2. **Compare methods, not people.** A technical disagreement is not a contest of credentials, intelligence, or personal worth.
3. **Keep more than one approach possible.** Do not assume that one model, formula, framework, or implementation is universally best.
4. **Use evidence proportionate to the claim.** State what has been demonstrated, what has only been explored, and what remains unknown.
5. **Test the actual work.** A plausible equation, a working program, or a passing test suite does not by itself establish that a method is fit for every intended use.
6. **Explain trade-offs.** Include relevant costs, constraints, precision, complexity, data requirements, and failure conditions.
7. **Make results reproducible where possible.** Share the method, versions, inputs or lawful retrieval steps, configuration, and instructions needed to examine the result.
8. **Report inconvenient findings.** Do not hide failed tests, negative results, uncertainty, or material limitations.
9. **Turn useful findings into reusable work.** Where appropriate, develop research into documented models, code, tools, and implementation guidance.
10. **Respect contributors and rights.** Give accurate credit, review ideas constructively, and follow the repository's licensing, data, and conduct policies.

## 6. How OFRL examines a method

The purpose of comparison is to learn what a method can and cannot do in a defined setting. It is not to produce a universal ranking of people or approaches.

A useful examination should make the following clear:

| Question | What we need to understand |
|---|---|
| What problem is being addressed? | Intended use, objective, scope, and relevant users |
| Under what conditions? | Assumptions, data, market or business environment, and constraints |
| How does the method work? | Theory, equations, logic, implementation, and key parameters |
| What does it claim to achieve? | Measurable criteria and the evidence required |
| What alternatives exist? | Relevant established or competing approaches |
| What do the tests show? | Correctness, sensitivity, robustness, failure modes, and uncertainty |
| What is useful in practice? | Applicability, trade-offs, operational requirements, and limitations |
| What should happen next? | Retain, revise, combine, test further, document, or discontinue the approach |

Not every project will answer every question at the same level of detail. The depth of examination must match the importance and risk of the claims being made. Project-specific requirements are defined in the Research Standards and the relevant project documentation.

## 7. What meaningful progress looks like

OFRL should be judged by the quality and usefulness of the work it helps create, not by repository activity, popularity, or the number of competing opinions.

Meaningful progress includes:

- A method that becomes clearer because its assumptions and mechanics are documented.
- A model that becomes more reliable because its calculations and edge cases are tested.
- An alternative approach that reveals a useful trade-off or works better under a defined set of conditions.
- A failed experiment that prevents an unsupported conclusion from being repeated.
- A research implementation that another person can reproduce, adapt, or independently challenge.
- A reusable software component or resource that solves a real problem.
- A contributor who can take an existing idea further and clearly identify what was changed or added.

A project does not need to produce a positive result to be valuable. It needs to produce an honest and useful contribution to understanding or practice.

## 8. Collaboration and governance

OFRL is founder-led at its current stage. The founder sets the overall research direction, selects and scopes projects, maintains the publication and disclosure standards, and takes responsibility for the decisions made under the OFRL name.

Founder-led does not mean that the founder's methods or opinions are beyond challenge. Contributors are welcome to question assumptions, propose different methods, provide independent implementations, reproduce results, and recommend changes. The quality of the reasoning and evidence matters more than agreement with the founder.

An open contribution is not an automatic acceptance. Work is reviewed for relevance, clarity, correctness, evidence, reproducibility, licensing, security, and maintainability as appropriate to its scope. A project may retain multiple documented approaches when they serve different purposes or when evidence does not support choosing one over another.

As the contributor community and project portfolio grow, OFRL may introduce documented project maintainers, review responsibilities, and decision procedures. Changes to governance should be explicit, proportionate, and recorded.

See [CONTRIBUTING.md](CONTRIBUTING.md), [RESEARCH_CHARTER.md](RESEARCH_CHARTER.md), and [RESEARCH_STANDARDS.md](RESEARCH_STANDARDS.md) for operational requirements.

## 9. What OFRL is not

OFRL is not:

- A competition to rank people by intelligence, credentials, seniority, or trading performance.
- A campaign to prove that one formula, model, or school of thought is always right.
- A place where every proposed idea is accepted without examination.
- A claim that all methods are equally correct, robust, or useful.
- A repository for confidential, employer-owned, client-owned, or unauthorised proprietary material.
- A provider of personalised investment advice, performance guarantees, or trading signals.

The lab is open to different ideas while remaining accountable for the quality and integrity of the work published under its name.

## 10. A note from the founder

I do not expect everyone who contributes to OFRL to think like me or use the same methods I use. I expect us to be able to explain our thinking, listen to a different approach, examine the evidence, and be willing to improve our work when we find a better way.

There will be times when different methods produce different results. There will also be times when one approach is more suitable than another for a particular objective or set of constraints. We should be willing to understand both situations rather than turn every disagreement into a question of who is right or who is better.

I started OFRL to create something that people can contribute to and take further—not something that ends with my own work. What matters to me is that the work becomes more useful, more understandable, and more capable of helping the next person build something better.

**We do not have to think alike to make progress together. We do have to take the work seriously.**
